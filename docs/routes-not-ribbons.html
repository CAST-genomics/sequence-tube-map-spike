Routes,
Not Ribbons

A pangenome tube map draws 466 haplotype strands and asks a researcher to read them. The data underneath holds between 4 and 198 distinct assertions. Five natural groupings are already sitting in the files, unused — and one of them turns out to be a small correction to what the whole project assumed the colours meant.

The language, first

This document uses the domain's own words, because using anything else would make it harder to check against the data. Here is what each one means. Nothing below assumes any of it.

Legend
pangenome
Not one reference genome but many, held together in a single structure — so that “the genome” becomes a population rather than a string.
pangenome graph
The data structure that does it. A directed graph whose nodes are stretches of DNA sequence and whose edges say which stretches can follow which. Shared sequence is stored once; where individuals differ, the graph branches and rejoins.
node
One vertex of that graph: a run of sequence, from 1 base to 72,000, that every individual carrying it carries identically. The choke points in the picture are nodes that everyone passes through.
haplotype
One individual's one copy of a region. Humans carry two copies of most of the genome — one from each parent — so a sample (a person) contributes two haplotypes. Each strand in the tube map is one haplotype's walk through the graph.
allele
A particular version of a stretch of sequence — one of the alternatives available at a place where individuals differ. In graph terms: which branch you take. This document mostly says “route” instead, because that is literally what the data holds.
route (this document's term)
The set of nodes one haplotype traverses. Two haplotypes on the same route walked the same path through the graph and are, at this scale, the same object.
PCLAI
The upstream model that places each haplotype at a 2-D coordinate summarising its ancestry, and ships a colour encoding that coordinate. The tube map does not choose strand colours; it reads this field.
local ancestry
The observation that ancestry is not one label per person. Different stretches of one person's genome can descend from different ancestral populations, so the coordinate is properly a function of position, not just of the individual.
PanSN naming
The convention behind names like HG00097#1#CM094060.1 — sample, then which of the two copies, then the contig it came from. Sample identity and haplotype index are therefore free to read.
homozygous / heterozygous
A sample whose two haplotypes are the same over a region is homozygous there; different, heterozygous. Below this is called a concordant or discordant pair, since “same” here means “same route”.
silhouette score
Standard clustering diagnostic, −1 to 1. Above ~0.7 means the clusters are tight and well separated — i.e. the grouping is in the data, not imposed by the algorithm.
AMI
Adjusted mutual information, 0 to 1. How much knowing one grouping tells you about another. 0 means independent.
impainted
A literal value the confidence field takes, in place of a number — a placement filled in rather than inferred. Spelled as it appears in the data.
§

The question

The tube map's standing problem is that strands cannot be told apart: they run parallel in dense bundles, hundreds of them share a colour exactly, and at fit-to-screen 464 of them land on about 177 rows of pixels. Every strategy the project has tried so far starts from one strand and tries to pull it out of the crowd.

This survey asks the opposite question. Before you try to separate one strand from the crowd, how much of the crowd is actually there? The answer changes what is worth building, because a great deal of the apparent complexity is not complexity — it is repetition drawn at full price.

Why it matters. The failure at fit-to-screen is a pixel budget, not a rendering flaw. Nothing subtle fixes it. Reducing the number of things drawn is the only move that attacks it at the cause.

The same collapse appears one level down, inside a single node's expansion. Reconstructing routes from the tube map SVGs in public/ — a coarser method, since node membership there has to be inferred from geometry — 369 strands reduce to 47 routes, 378 to 118, and 464 to 112, with the top five routes carrying 45–69% of the strands in each.

chr6 deserves its highlight. 198 routes, 98 of them travelled by a single haplotype, no route above 3%. The payoff from grouping is a property of the locus, not a constant — and a locus where grouping buys nothing is itself a finding the researcher should be handed, not one they should have to discover by squinting at a wall of ribbons.

Read the exception this way: loci differ in kind, not just in size. A viewer that says which kind you have landed in is doing real work before you touch anything.

02

Most nodes separate nobody

Bucket the nodes of each locus by how many haplotypes carry them. A node carried by everyone is a choke point — by construction it distinguishes no one, and drawing it at full weight spends ink on a fact that is already known. A node carried by 10–90% is where the population actually divides.

The contrast sharpens at fine granularity. In the tube map SVG for node 5520+, 171 of 274 nodes are variable against only 2 universal — a hypervariable neighbourhood. Its neighbour 5514+ is 242 universal and 256 common: a quiet backbone with a handful of bubbles. These are qualitatively different objects and the current picture presents them identically.

Free affordance. A frequency threshold that hides universal nodes costs no information whatsoever. It cannot hide a distinction, because those nodes hold none.

03

Five ancestry groups, and they are crisp

The PCLAI coordinates are not diffuse. Clustering them with k-means and letting the silhouette score choose k gives k = 5 in all six loci, at silhouette 0.89–0.93. That is an unusually clean separation: five buckets with no modelling judgement left to make, and the same five however the data is sliced.

Note what the figure also shows. The two large lobes at lower right are close enough that neighbouring points receive colours a single unit apart in one channel. Position separates them; colour, on its own, cannot. That is the existing problem restated in its own coordinate system.

Cheapest of the five. It needs one field the band parser does not currently read, and a string split.

05

Not every colour is a measurement

The confidence field beside each coordinate is not simply a number. It takes the literal value impainted — a filled-in rather than inferred placement — and the coordinate object is sometimes empty, which is the case the renderer already draws in grey. Both are common, and how common varies enormously by locus.

Counts are per observation — one haplotype at one node — not per haplotype. In chr6, more than a third of all observations have no placement at all. Downstream, the same swing shows up between two tube map SVGs of adjacent nodes: 6 unplaced strands in one, 99 in the other.

A researcher reading colour as ancestry is entitled to know which strands are carrying a filled-in placement, a low-confidence one, or none. This is a grouping in the strict sense — a partition of the strands — and it is the one with the clearest duty attached.

§

Where routes and ancestry meet

Cross-tabulating route against ancestry cluster gives an AMI of 0.02 to 0.19 across the six loci. Weak — and the weakness is the interesting part. The common routes draw from all five clusters roughly in proportion: shared structure is shared by everyone, which is what a pangenome is for.

The signal lives in the tail. Two to four of the sizeable routes in each locus are at least 90% a single ancestry cluster. In the 600 bp tube map SVG, the top route splits 14 / 26 / 40 / 49 / 8 across the five clusters — essentially population-agnostic — while three smaller routes, carrying 18, 9 and 8 strands, come almost entirely from one.

The filter this implies

Show me the routes carried by only one ancestry group. That takes a screen of ribbons to a short list of claims, and it is not a rendering trick — it is the picture answering a question that was asked of it.

§

A correction the survey forces

The project has been treating the PCLAI coordinate, and the colour derived from it, as a property of the haplotype. In the source it is a property of the haplotype at a node: the field sits inside each node's assembly metadata, and a haplotype appearing at 45 nodes carries 45 coordinates. That is local ancestry along the genome, which is what it ought to be — it is simply not what the downstream reading assumed.

In practice this changes almost nothing, and occasionally changes everything.

Drift is how far one haplotype's coordinates spread across a locus, as a fraction of the whole cloud's diameter. The median is three to five parts in a thousand — invisible. 410 of 460 haplotypes in cici receive more than one colour across the locus, and the median worst difference between them is 2 units in 255, which is below the threshold at which the colour encoding collides with itself anyway.

Then there is HG02004#2 in chr6. Across two nodes it moves 43% of the cloud's diameter and its colour shifts 101 units in a channel. That haplotype changes ancestry group inside the window. It is a real event, it is rare — 0 to 4 haplotypes per locus — and the current picture renders it as one ribbon among 466 that quietly changes hue.

The rarest bucket found here

“Haplotypes that change ancestry group within this window.” Zero to four strands out of 466. This grouping was not known to be available, and no existing affordance would ever surface it.

One consequence for anything built next: each tube map SVG in public/ covers a single node, which is why a haplotype has exactly one coordinate in each of them. That is a property of the window, not of the haplotype, and nothing should be built on it.

The original question, answered precisely: a haplotype name determines its coordinate within one node's view, but the same name carries a different coordinate at the next node — and once in a few hundred, a materially different one.

Method, and what would break it

Every figure comes from scripts/strand_grouping_survey.py run over the six datasets in pgb/public/datasets/api-v3 — PGB's own source data, so node membership is read, not inferred. The secondary numbers quoted from the tube map SVGs in public/ are reconstructed from rendered geometry by matching each strand's segments against node bounding boxes, and should be treated as approximate.

Four things that would change the conclusions:

Routes are scale-dependent. A route here is a set of nodes at the dataset's own granularity. Two haplotypes identical at that level can differ inside a node — the SVG survey shows exactly that happening. Any affordance built on routes has to state at what scale it means “the same”.

Thickness would be a new claim. Today a band's thickness means one haplotype. Making it mean n haplotypes changes what the map asserts, and that has to be deliberate rather than a side effect of collapsing.

The viewer cannot compute this yet. These numbers come from the dataset JSON. The band parser does not currently read haplotype names, let alone node membership. Whether routes are computed client-side or arrive from PGB is undecided, and is the first question, not the last.

Six loci is six loci. The k = 5 clustering is remarkably stable across all of them, and the route collapse holds in five. Neither is a claim about the genome.
