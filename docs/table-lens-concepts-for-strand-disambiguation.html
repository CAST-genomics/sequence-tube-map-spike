A Lens With Its
Handle Elsewhere

At fit-to-screen a haplotype strand is 0.19 css pixels tall and nothing can be found in the bundle. The Table Lens answered this in 1994 by expanding the row you point at. This proposes the same expansion with the pointing moved into a second panel — a PCLAI scatter where every strand has an address, where the whole bounded space is on screen at once, and where resolution is something you can buy.

§

The claim, in one paragraph

Selection happens in a PCLAI scatter plot, not in the tube map. The strand chosen there is drawn in the map at a screen-space minimum thickness — a floor, not a multiplier — so that a sub-pixel band becomes findable. The floor is inert at every zoom where the band is already thicker than it, and it releases the instant the cursor leaves the chart. The map at rest is never distorted.

Three things make this different from the treatment tried and removed on 14 August, and each is argued below: the distortion is geometric rather than tonal, the handle is outside the distorted view, and the whole thing is self-annulling — it disappears precisely where it would begin to lie.

Every number quoted here is from an existing measurement in this repo. Nothing in this document has been rendered, and by constraint 5 nothing here is settled until it has been.
01

The regime that defeats everything else

Fit-to-screen on 5520+: 464 strands land on about 177 device rows, a band is 0.19 css pixels tall, and roughly two and a half bands share every device pixel row. The strip's aspect is 14:1 to 28:1 and is set by the genome, so there is no vertical zoom to spend. This is the bound DISAMBIGUATING-STRANDS.md states as constraint 3: legibility at fit is bounded by pixels, not by cleverness.

Feeler mode (#39) works from about one css pixel per band upward and fails here, measured and photographed. That is not a defect in the treatment. It is the treatment meeting an arithmetic wall.

What makes the wall worth attacking rather than conceding: fit is the only view in which constraint 2 is satisfiable at all. Constraint 2 asks for the whole strand rather than the visible part, and at working zoom a strand crosses tens of screens. So the one view where a haplotype's entire path is present is the one view where it cannot be seen. Zoomed in you can tell strands apart and cannot follow them; at fit you could follow them and cannot tell them apart. Repairing fit is not polishing the worst case — it is completing the only view that answers the tracing question.

A discrepancy worth resolving. RENDERING.md reports 2.6 strands per device pixel row at fit on 5520+ (464 / 177, consistent with 0.19 css px at dpr 2); the feeler note reports 5.7 for the same document. Figures here use 2.6. The conclusions do not turn on which is right, but one of them is.
02

What Rao and Card built, and which half we take

The Table Lens (Rao & Card, CHI '94, Xerox PARC) put a large table on one screen by compressing every row to a pixel or two and expanding only the rows under a degree-of-interest function. Focus and context in one surface, with three properties worth naming because we keep two of them and break the third.

PropertyTable Lens, 1994Here
Focus is expanded in place Focal rows grow; the rest stay compressed. Kept. The strand grows about its own centreline; its position stays truthful.
Distortion is reversible and continuous The lens follows the pointer and releases. Kept, and load-bearing. The release is what makes the distortion honest.
The handle is in the distorted view You click the row you want, in the table itself. Broken deliberately. The handle is a second panel.
Space is conserved Focus expands, context compresses; total height constant. Open. Both variants are cheap here — see §06.
Rao, R. and Card, S. K., The Table Lens: Merging Graphical and Symbolic Representations in an Interactive Focus+Context Visualization for Tabular Information, CHI '94. The wider family — Furnas' fisheye views, degree-of-interest — has one consistent finding worth inheriting: spatial distortion is well tolerated when it is reversible and under direct user control, and intolerable when it persists.
03

Why the handle moves out of the view

A table has rows, and a row is an address. That is what makes direct manipulation work in the original: the thing you want to expand is already an addressable object under your cursor.

The tube map has no such index. Vertical order here is layout, not identity — it is where the server routed a ribbon so the bundle reads, and it changes along the strip. There is no row 12. At fit there is not even a pointable target: 2.6 strands share the pixel under the cursor, so a click is a lottery among them. Direct manipulation fails for the same arithmetic reason the display does, and no amount of picking precision (#38) repairs it, because the ambiguity is in the input device's resolution, not the pick.

So the handle has to live where the strands do have addresses. Four consequences follow, and the third is the one that decides it.

1 · The map at rest is never distorted

With hover-on-the-map you are always deforming the thing you are looking at. With hover-on-the-chart, the deformation occurs only while attention is deliberately in the other panel; the strip is truthful whenever it is the object of study. This is a strictly better arrangement and it is available only because the handle moved.

2 · Selection becomes strand-global rather than cursor-local

DISAMBIGUATING-STRANDS.md records the same caveat against picking twice: it answers what is under the cursor, not what you are looking at three screens to the right. A dot in the chart is not at a position along the strip, so the caveat does not arise. You have named a haplotype, not touched a band.

3 · The chart has an unspent resolution budget, and the strip has none

Strategy C concluded that no PCLAI-derived channel can individuate a strand. That is true of a fixed-size rendering, and the reason is a resolution budget — which a scatter plot, unlike the strip, can simply buy.

From the survey: a haplotype's median nearest neighbour sits 0.064–0.088% of the cloud's diameter away. To place a median pair 4 px apart the plot must be about 4 / 0.0007 ≈ 5,700 px across — roughly 10× zoom on a 600 px panel. Not exotic. Pan and zoom in a scatter, costing nothing and losing nothing. The survey's own table corroborates the scaling: doubling effective resolution took discriminable points from 78–99 to 209–233.

The strip cannot be magnified in the axis that matters. The chart can be magnified in both. That asymmetry is the whole argument for moving the handle, and it is not a matter of taste.

4 · The per-node coordinate becomes a feature

Strategy C records, as a correction, that PCLAI is a property of the haplotype at a node rather than of the haplotype. Median drift across a locus is 0.3–0.5% of the cloud — invisible. In a linked chart that footnote becomes behaviour: let the cloud be a function of the map's current viewport, and scrolling the strip settles the dots into their coordinates for the nodes now on screen.

For 456 of 460 haplotypes nothing visible happens, which is the honest picture of locally stable ancestry. And then HG02004#2 in chr6 leaps 43% of the cloud's diameter across two nodes. That is a real biological event which the current picture renders as one hairline among 466 quietly changing hue by 101/255. In a linked chart it is the only thing moving.

This is the rarest bucket the survey found — 0 to 4 haplotypes per locus — and no affordance in the strategy document surfaces it. Here it falls out of the design without being designed for.
04

The chart is a navigator for the other axis

The reason this arrangement should feel familiar is that the project has already built half of it. The map has two extents a reader has to traverse — position along the genome, and position in the population — and only one of them has an overview.

The navigator (#36) covers the first. Its record states its job in one line: "the navigator's entire job is to say where you are, and it does that with the rect, so the rect has to be findable at the zoom where being lost is possible." That is exactly what a PCLAI scatter does for the second extent. The coordinate space is bounded, fully known, and every haplotype in the document lands somewhere in it. Hovering a dot is not only a selection — it is a position report: which lobe, how near its edge, how far from everything else.

Quotation from notes/2026-08-14-navigator-thumbnail-aspect.md, which settled THUMBNAIL_WIDTH = 720 by rendering it against 5520+ and 5514+ rather than reasoning about it. The same method applies here, and the same warning: that note also records the 360 px width having been calibrated against the 600 bp fixture and being wrong for it (constraint 6).

So the proposal is not a new kind of widget bolted to the side. It is the completion of a pattern this project already accepted, applied to the axis that was left uncovered.

Read the fourth and fifth rows together. The two overviews are essential in precisely opposite regimes. Neither is a redundant copy of the other, and the lens is needed exactly where the navigator has nothing to do. That is a strong argument that the chart is not chrome creep: it is the missing member of a pair.

What boundedness buys that a list cannot

Indirect selection has been on the table since #50 as a column of 466 pills. Both it and the chart are indirect selection; the difference is that a bounded space can be traversed and a list can only be scrolled.

Nothing is hidden. A 466-row list has a bottom you cannot see. The strip has 463 strands you cannot tell apart. The chart puts every selectable object on screen simultaneously, so a researcher can know they have seen the whole population rather than hoping so. That guarantee is not available anywhere else in this viewer.

A sweep can be systematic and can finish. In a bounded space you can work lobe by lobe, edge to edge, and tell when you are done. A list requires a name you do not yet have, which is precisely the situation the disambiguation problem describes. The list wins when you know who you are looking for; the chart wins when you do not.

The recursion, and the rule it suggests

§03 argues the chart's zoom is load-bearing — about 10× to resolve a lobe. The moment it zooms, the chart inherits the navigator's own problem: being lost inside a magnified space. It therefore needs the navigator's own answer, an overview inset with a viewport rect, and this project has already solved that once and knows what it costs. A navigator for the navigator is not a joke; it is the same widget at a different scale.

One rule, closing an open question

Every overview reflects the current selection. #39's fourth open question — that an emphasized strand does not appear in the navigator, because the thumbnail is baked once per document — stops being a loose end and becomes an instance of a rule the chart needs anyway. Both overviews are one render each. Selection is the shared object (§09), and every surface that claims to show the whole of something has to show the selection too, or it is lying about being an overview.

The two panels, and the loop between them

06

The floor, stated precisely

Not draw the selected strand 2 px tall. Rather, for the emphasized strand only:

thickness = max(natural_thickness, floor_px)

with floor_px somewhere around 1.5–2 css pixels, growing symmetrically about the band's centreline so its position stays exactly truthful even as its extent does not.

The property that makes this defensible is that it is self-annulling. Above the floor the clamp does nothing and the map is precisely as honest as it is today. Below it, the clamp is the entire effect — and below it is exactly the regime where the neighbours being overdrawn were never resolvable in the first place. The distortion exists only where it distorts nothing legible, and disappears the moment it would begin to lie.

Overdraw, or conserve space?

Rao and Card conserved total height: expanding a focal row compressed the others. Both variants are cheap here, and the arithmetic is worth having before the choice is made.

VariantWhat it costs at fitVerdict
Overdraw The floored strand covers about 9 neighbours, which at 0.19 css px each were not resolvable anyway. Every other strand stays pinned. Recommended for fit. Nothing readable is lost and nothing moves.
Conserve (compress the rest) The 8 bands of room, spread over 456 neighbours, is 1.75% compression each — negligible. But every strand's y shifts, and the bundle breathes on every hover. Cheaper than expected. The objection is not cost, it is that vertical position is the low-frequency cue still working at fit.
Worth testing both, because the intuition here is weak and the cost of being wrong is one shader constant. Constraint 5.
07

Why the 14 August removal does not bind this

notes/2026-08-14-feeler-mode-on-the-gpu.md records a floor of ink tried and removed, and read quickly it reads like this proposal already failing. It is not the same treatment, and the difference is the whole of §06.

What was tried was tonal: draw the emphasized band as though it were a pixel thick so it composites at full colour instead of a fraction of it. The geometry never changed. A fully-opaque band 0.19 css px tall still lands inside a device row it shares with others, so it still contributes roughly a third of that row's ink. Full alpha on partial coverage is still partial coverage. It turned a 59%-alpha hairline into a solid one on the fixture and — exactly as the note reports — bought nothing at fit on 5520+.

What is proposed is geometric: the band owns three or four device rows outright. This is not a better version of the failed experiment; it is a different quantity, an order of magnitude apart. Figure 3 is that difference drawn.

The other removal reason, met head on

The note's first reason was the stronger one, and it applies here too: a band emitting more ink than the document gave it is brightening by any observable test, and the defence that it only ever reached the ink a pixel-tall band would have had is an argument about the mechanism, not about what the eye sees. That is correct, and geometric dilation emits more ink than tonal restoration did, not less. There is no rules-lawyer escape here and none should be attempted.

What can be said is narrower and, I think, sufficient. Story 30's rule — recede the others rather than brighten the one — was derived from a perceptual finding: against hundreds of saturated neighbours, brightening does not read. It is a rule about what works, not a prohibition on ink. A treatment that demonstrably does read is not condemned by a rule whose justification is non-reading; and a treatment that does not read is killed by the second removal reason anyway. So the rule can be tested rather than obeyed, and one of the two outcomes settles it either way.

Two further points. The lens does not replace the recede — the crowd still ghosts, as #39 built it and as translucency won. And extent is not the saturated channel; ink is. Adding the one channel that is not yet contended is not the move story 30 examined.

If it works, amend the rule

Better to write at fit, receding alone is insufficient and extent is the added channel into SPEC.md story 30 than to argue that a nine-fold dilation is not brightening. If the picture succeeds, the honest outcome is a qualified rule, not a finessed one.

08

The precedent is already in the renderer

RENDERING.md states a doctrine for exactly this class of correction, and then an instance of it:

"A feature's size is corrected only where scale destroys information the feature carries." And: uPad "grows a band to cover a device pixel… The correction is a floor, never a fixed size: above one pixel uPad does nothing and thickness 15 scales freely, 0.19 css px at fit to 38 at 200×."

So the mechanism proposed here already exists in the shader, as a global correction, framed in the same words — a floor, inert above one pixel. What the lens adds is a second, higher floor applied to one strand under a gesture. The doctrine admits it on its own terms: at fit, scale destroys the strand's identity, and identity is information the feature carries. uPad protects colour from being lost to sampling; the lens protects identity from the same thing.

This is also the cleanest available answer to Tufte's Lie Factor, which the proposal plainly incurs: a strand drawn 1.75 css px where the document says 0.19 has a lie factor near 9. What pays for it is that the deception is reversible, user-caused, and watched. The user sees the band deflate to a hairline on release, which teaches its true size more vividly than never inflating it would. Nobody believes a magnifying glass changed the object. A persistent nine-fold dilation would be indefensible; a transient one under a live gesture is an instrument.

This is the data-encoded / interaction-state distinction the strategy document does not yet draw. The shipped RGB is shared vocabulary with PGB's chart and 3D graph and is sacred (constraint 1). Emphasis, ghosting and this floor assert nothing about the data and exist only while a gesture holds. Constraint 1 binds the first and has no business binding the second.
09

What would break it

Five things, in the order they are likely to bite.

RiskWhyWhat to do about it
Cardinality One strand at 9× costs 9 rows of 177. A lasso of 40 costs 360 and the map ceases to exist. Cap the floored set at 3–5. Degrade a larger selection to the recede alone, or to an envelope. Decide before the lasso exists.
Dilation may not suffice alone One solid line among 464 saturated ribbons may still not pop. Against a ghosted crowd it should be trivial. Test floor + recede together, never floor alone. If it still fails, the outline/halo candidate is next, not a larger floor.
The dots collide too At 1× the lobes are a smear and a hover picks an arbitrary member. The zoom is load-bearing, not a nicety. Report n dots under the cursor rather than silently choosing one. Make "you are in a smear, magnify" legible.
Hover gives up comparison #39 already learned that a following emphasis cannot accumulate a set. Hover to explore, lasso to commit. The chart is a far better home for an accumulated set than the strip was.
Ownership PGB already draws this chart from the same field. Picking, the pill list (#50), a route menu and the chart are all producers of one selection. Make selection a first-class shared object on day one, or four affordances will each invent their own.

One non-risk worth recording: a screen-space-constant thickness is more stable under pan than a natural sub-pixel band, which shimmers as it resamples. The floored strand should be the calmest thing on screen.

§

What this does not claim

It does not repeal constraint 3. The pixel budget is real and this is a way of spending nine strands' worth of it on one strand, under a gesture, in the one regime where the other eight were unreadable. That is a trade, not a repeal.

It does not compete with Strategy C. Grouping reduces how many things are drawn; the lens finds one thing among what is drawn. Twenty-one routes at fit are each above a pixel and need no lens at all — which is the best argument for building both, and for building the lens knowing it may become unnecessary at the loci where grouping pays.

And nothing here has been rendered. Every number is quoted from a prior measurement in this repository; the one number this proposal turns on — whether a floored strand is findable at fit on 5520+ — does not exist yet, and this project's own rule is that counts propose and the screen decides.
