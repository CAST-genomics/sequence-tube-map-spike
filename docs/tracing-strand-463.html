The subject

A strand is one individual's one copy of a chromosome, drawn as a lane that runs the width of the map. It is not one shape. Strand 463 is 63 separate drawables — 26 flat <rect> runs and 37 curved <path> transitions — scattered through a 14.2 MB document, united only by the attribute trackID="463".

It was picked for two reasons and no others. It is the first drawable in the document, so its bytes are the first bytes the parser sees; and its id lands in the second row of the appearance table, so the texel address is worth reading rather than trivially zero. Its colour, rgb(0, 229, 188), is the PCLAI ancestry encoding the upstream document assigned it and nothing in this renderer ever changes.

Reading the d attribute

That string is a program for a pen. Each capital letter is one instruction and the numbers after it are its arguments, in absolute document coordinates with y pointing down. The whole tube-map grammar uses four instructions, and this band uses all of them.

InstructionArgsWhat the pen does
M x y2Move to. Lift the pen and put it at (x, y). Draws nothing — it only sets where the outline starts.
C x₁ y₁ x₂ y₂ x y6Cubic curve to. Draw a cubic Bézier from wherever the pen already is to (x, y), bent on the way by two control points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂).
V y1Vertical line to. Straight line from the pen to (its own x, y). One argument, because x does not change.
Z0Close. Straight line back to wherever M put the pen, sealing the outline so it can be filled.
 Table 1. Capital letters mean absolute coordinates; the lowercase forms (m, c, v) mean relative ones and never appear in these documents.

So the band's d attribute, one instruction to a stanza, with the pen's position before and after each:

It is one closed loop, not two strokes: along the top, down the right end, back along the bottom, up the left end. What gets painted is the region enclosed. The renderer never draws that loop — it reads four corners and two control abscissae out of it and rebuilds the enclosed region as a triangle strip.

A cubic is four points, and the curve touches two of them

A cubic Bézier is defined by four points P₀ P₁ P₂ P₃, swept by a parameter t running 0 to 1:

The cubic Bézier, in general 
B(t) = (1−t)³·P₀  +  3(1−t)²t·P₁  +  3(1−t)t²·P₂  +  t³·P₃

B(0) = P₀          B(1) = P₃          P₁ and P₂ are never reached

Only the two endpoints lie on the curve. P₁ and P₂ are weights: the curve leaves P₀ heading straight at P₁, arrives at P₃ coming straight from P₂, and bends between. And the C instruction writes only three of the four points — six numbers, not eight. P₀ is wherever the pen already was, which is exactly what makes the notation feel like it is missing something. It is.

The four points of this band's upper edge 
P₀ = (77, 6965)                  · from the pen — the M, not the C
P₁ = (1159.706982085443, 6965)   · written
P₂ = (1159.706982085443, 6875)   · written
P₃ = (1626.5714285714287, 6875)  · written

Two things about those four points are peculiar to this document's grammar, and everything the renderer does downstream depends on both.

The control points share an x. P₁ and P₂ both sit at 1159.706982085443 — one vertical line. And each copies its neighbour's y: P₁ is level with P₀, P₂ is level with P₃. Which means the tangents at both ends are dead horizontal —

Why the grammar is shaped this way 
tangent at P₀ = 3·(P₁ − P₀) = (3248.121, 0)     · horizontal
tangent at P₃ = 3·(P₃ − P₂) = (1400.593, 0)     · horizontal

A curved transition therefore leaves and arrives perfectly level, which is what lets it butt against a flat <rect> run with no visible kink. The strand is drawn as alternating flat runs and lifts, and this is the constraint that makes the seams invisible.

The same two properties are what collapse the arithmetic. Substitute P₁.y = P₀.y and P₂.y = P₃.y into B(t) and the y half reduces to smoothstep; normalise the x half by the span, writing u = (P₁.x − P₀.x) / width, and the four points become one number:

The same curve, after the grammar is applied 
u = (1159.706982085443 − 77) / 1549.5714285714287 = 0.6987138

x(t) = x₀ + width · (3u·t·(1−t) + t³)
y(t) = y₀ + (y₁ − y₀) · (3t² − 2t³)                · smoothstep

t = 0.5   →   (1082.726665, 6920.000000)   · identical to B(0.5), to the last digit

Note what t = 0.5 is and is not. It is exactly halfway in y — 6920 is the mean of 6965 and 6875 — and it is 64.9% of the way along in x, where halfway would be 851.8. That asymmetry is the entire content of u, and it is the one number per edge the renderer keeps.

Fifteen numbers appear in that d attribute. Only six are information: 77, 6965, 1626.5714285714287, 6875, and the two control abscissae. Everything else is the grammar restating them.

Not parsed with DOMParser. parseBands.ts runs one regular expression over the raw response text. Building 40,442 DOM nodes is the exact cost this renderer exists to escape, and doing it once at load to avoid it later would be self-defeating.

The parse: six floats and an id

The document's frame is not the renderer's. SVG's y points down from the viewBox corner; three.js's y points up from a camera at the origin. Both differences are resolved here, once, and nothing downstream knows the source was ever SVG.

The two control abscissae differ — by 1.993 document units, the third decimal place of the fraction. That difference is the whole reason a band is not a stroked curve: the upper and lower edges are separate cubics, and the shortcut "offset the top edge downward by 15" would be measurably wrong. §06 shows how wrong.

Why the control point is stored as a fraction

The document is 108,983 units wide, and 5514+ is 177,994. At that magnitude a float32 ulp is 0.0156 units — enough to move a control point visibly inside a span of a few hundred. Normalising in double and storing 0.6987138 confines the large magnitude to x0 alone. Rounded to float32 the six values become −54414.28516, −3132.5, 1549.571411, −3042.5, 0.69871384, 0.69999999.

The flat runs get the same treatment. A <rect> has no control points, so the parser gives it uTop = uBot = 0.5 — any abscissa reproduces a horizontal line — and y1 = y0. There is one band type on the GPU, not two, and no branch anywhere distinguishes them.

04

The vertex shader places 130 vertices

There is one mesh in the scene and it has no positions. It is a ladder: 65 rungs at even values of t, two vertices each, 128 triangles, 384 indices — uploaded once, shared by every band in every document. The per-vertex attribute is aParam = (t, side), and that is all.

The shader runs 130 times for strand 463's band #17322, and 5,257,460 times for the document. Here is what it computes at the middle rung, t = 0.5, longhand.

Rung 32 · aParam = (0.5, 0) · the upper edge 
u = mix(0.6987138, 0.7000000, 0)                      = 0.6987138
x = −54414.2857 + 1549.5714 · (3·0.6987138·0.5·0.5 + 0.125)
  = −54414.2857 + 1549.5714 · 0.64903535              = −53408.559
y = mix(−3132.5, −3042.5, smoothstep(0.5)) − 0 · 15     = −3087.500
Rung 32 · aParam = (0.5, 1) · the lower edge 
u = mix(0.6987138, 0.7000000, 1)                      = 0.7000000
x = −54414.2857 + 1549.5714 · (3·0.7·0.5·0.5 + 0.125)
  = −54414.2857 + 1549.5714 · 0.65000000              = −53407.065
y = mix(−3132.5, −3042.5, smoothstep(0.5)) − 1 · 15     = −3102.500

The two vertices of one rung are 1.495 units apart in x. They share a t and they do not share an x — which is the geometric content of "the two edges are not translates of each other" — a difference in the third decimal place of the input, and in whole units of the output.

The y line is the load-bearing simplification. The cubic's control ordinates are copies of its endpoints, so the ordinate collapses to smoothstep — there is no Bézier evaluation in y anywhere in this renderer. And x(0) = 0, x(1) = 1 for every u, so the two edges meet exactly at both ends however far their control abscissae diverge, and the band closes without a seam.

All 65 rungs, and where they land

Placing rungs at even t rather than even x is a deliberate refusal to root-find in the vertex shader: the two edges have different u, so they do not share an x at equal t, and asking for even spacing in x would mean inverting the cubic 130 times per band per frame. The cost of refusing is that rungs bunch — here by a factor of 3.2 between the widest and the narrowest step. Tessellation error follows curvature, not spacing, so it costs nothing.

The rasterizer picks one fragment

Put the camera on this band at a working zoom — 19.2× fit, which is 0.246645 CSS pixels per world unit at 1400×900 on a dpr-2 display. The band now occupies CSS pixels 508.9 to 891.1, a 382-pixel arc rising 22 pixels and 3.70 CSS pixels thick. One device pixel is 1 / (0.246645 × 2) = 2.0272 world units, and the shader is handed uHalfPixel = 1.0136 and uPad = 2.0272.

Take the quad between rungs 32 and 33 — 18 world units of span, about 4.4 CSS pixels wide — and within it the fragment whose centre sits at world x = −53400.0. The rasterizer hands the fragment shader three interpolated varyings and three flat ones:

What arrives at the fragment 
flat  vSpan     = (−54414.2857, −3132.5, 1549.5714, −3042.5)   · the instance, unchanged
flat  vControl  = (0.6987138, 0.7000000)
flat  vColor    = (0.0, 0.898, 0.737)
flat  vEmphasis = 1.0

vT is a linear interpolation along a curve — rung 32 sits at x −53408.559 with t 0.5, rung 33 at x −53390.620 with t 0.515625, and this fragment is 47.7% of the way between them. That makes vT wrong by 4.7 × 10⁻⁵ in t, which is 0.054 world units in x, which is 1/75 of a CSS pixel. Close. Not exact. §06 makes it exact.

The fragment's y is not used to decide anything yet. Rasterization only decided that a fragment runs here. Whether the band actually put ink in it, and how much, is not a rasterization question in this renderer — it is arithmetic, and it is next.

06

Two Newton steps, twice

The fragment needs to know where the band's two edges are at its own x, not at the rung it was interpolated from. So it inverts the cubic. Normalise the fragment's x into the span first:

The fragment's position along the band 
p = (−53400.0 − (−54414.2857)) / 1549.5714 = 0.6545589

Then two unrolled Newton iterations on f(t) = 3u·t·(1−t) + t³ − p, seeded with vT. The derivative f′(t) = 3u(1−2t) + 3t² bottoms out at 3u(1−u) > 0, so this cannot stall, and there is no branch and no early exit — every fragment does exactly the same work.

Upper edge · u = 0.6987138 · seed 0.50745482 
step 1   f = +3.490359e−05   f′ = 0.74128   →  t = 0.50740773
step 2   f = −1.272203e−09   f′ = 0.74133   →  t = 0.50740773   · unchanged to 8 places
Lower edge · u = 0.7000000 · same seed 
step 1   f = +9.993194e−04   f′ = 0.74122   →  t = 0.50610661
step 2   f = −1.052397e−06   f′ = 0.74278   →  t = 0.50610803

The lower edge starts 20× further from its answer, because the seed was interpolated along the upper edge's rungs, and still converges in two steps. That is the margin the method has.

The two edges are at different parameters at the same x — 0.50740773 and 0.50610803, a gap of 1.3 × 10⁻³. Evaluate y at each:

Where the band is, at world x = −53400 
yTop = mix(−3132.5, −3042.5, smoothstep(0.50740773))            = −3086.50003
yBot = mix(−3132.5, −3042.5, smoothstep(0.50610803)) − 15       = −3101.67546

vertical extent at this x                                       = 15.17543
Fifteen is not the answer

Every band in every document is height="15", and at this x the band is 15.175 units tall. A band's thickness measured vertically varies along its length, because its two edges are cubics with different control abscissae and reach a given x at different parameters. A renderer that stroked one curve at width 15 would be wrong here by 1.2% — sub-pixel at every zoom, and wrong at every one of them.

Horizontal coverage is deliberately ignored. The test measures only how much of the pixel's height the band fills. Bands lap their neighbours by a whole unit and are hundreds of units wide, so the two ends are interior to the strand and never a visible edge.

07

Coverage, down the whole column

Now the arithmetic that replaces antialiasing. The band runs from −3086.50003 to −3101.67546; each fragment's pixel box runs from its own centre ± 1.0136. Intersect the two intervals and divide by the pixel height.

The topmost fragment · centre y = −3085.8919 
hi = min(yTop, y + 1.0136) = min(−3086.50003, −3084.8783) = −3086.50003
lo = max(yBot, y − 1.0136) = max(−3101.67546, −3086.9055) = −3086.90550

coverage = (hi − lo) / 2.0272 = 0.40547 / 2.0272 = 0.2000

Not 0.25, not 0.5. MSAA at 4× can answer this question in quarters; this answers it in whatever fraction is true. Where the interval is empty the fragment discards and nothing is written at all.

The band is 3.70 CSS pixels thick here, so the column is ten fragments deep. Two of them are edges and one is empty:

Where the grid falls is arbitrary. The device-pixel lattice depends on where the camera sits, and here it was placed so the topmost fragment centre lands 0.608 units above the upper edge — which makes the top coverage exactly 0.2 and the arithmetic legible. Move the camera a third of a pixel and every number in this column changes; their sum does not.

The same band at fit-to-width

Everything above was at a working zoom. Open the document instead, and the camera fits 108,983 world units into 1400 CSS pixels: zoom = 0.0128461. Band #17322 is now 19.9 CSS pixels wide and 0.195 CSS pixels tall, and one device pixel is 38.922 world units — two and a half times the band's entire thickness.

Without intervention the band would now vanish. Not fade: vanish. Its geometry would lie entirely between two rows of fragment centres, no fragment would be generated, and the strand would silently not be drawn. That is what uPad is for.

The pad, at fit 
uPad = 38.922                                     · one device pixel, in world units

vertex y += (1 − 2·side) · uPad                   · upper edge up, lower edge down

drawn quad     : 15.175 + 2 · 38.922 = 93.0 units tall   · certain to catch a fragment
coverage still : (yTop − yBot) / 38.922 = 0.3899          · the band's true thickness

The pad grows the geometry and never the coverage test, which recomputes the edges from vSpan and vControl and knows nothing about the padding. It is a floor on the number of fragments, not a floor on the ink.

Whether 39% of a colour on a thread that thin is legible is a different question, and it is the one DISAMBIGUATING-STRANDS.md exists to answer.

Where the unbuilt normalisation would help most. At fit, 464 strands stack inside a few hundred pixels and the horizontal-coverage term the shader ignores stops being negligible for the shortest bands. The correct fix is to normalise coverage by the gradient; it is not built, and the reason it is not built is in the parent note.
