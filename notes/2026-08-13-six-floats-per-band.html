Six Floats Per Band

A GPU has no curve primitive. The map is nothing but curves — 464 haplotypes drawn as forty thousand separate pieces, stacked into a strip a hundred and fifty thousand units wide. What follows is how they get drawn: one draw call, six floats per piece, and no tessellator.

Written 13 August 2026 as a design, and revised 14 August after building it. The geometry held exactly. Several conclusions about antialiasing and about what would survive from the old viewer did not, and each is corrected in place below rather than quietly edited away.

01 The shape of the problem

The server sends an SVG. Inside it, every haplotype that crosses this stretch of the genome is drawn as a coloured ribbon running left to right, stepping up and down as it threads between sequence segments.

That ribbon is not one shape. It arrives already cut into pieces — a median of 28 of them per haplotype in the reference document, around 87 in the largest node measured — alternating strictly between a rectangle where the ribbon crosses a sequence segment and a curve where it travels between two. Each piece is a band: a closed shape bounded above and below by cubic curves, filled flat, no stroke. So the forty thousand figure counts shapes, not strands, and the fragmentation turns out to be the reason any of the rest works.

Drawing this through the DOM stopped working twice in two days — once at style invalidation, once at raster. So the pixels have to come from somewhere else. The obvious cost is that WebGL draws triangles, and a triangle is not a curve. The usual answer is to flatten each curve into short line segments and triangulate, which means choosing a tolerance, rebuilding geometry when the zoom changes, and living with faceting when you guess low.

None of that turned out to be necessary, because of what the data actually is.

02 Six floats

Every band path matches a single grammar. Not most — 127,101 out of 127,101, across every document the API would return: seventeen nodes, spans from 1 to 7,967 base pairs, 369 to 464 haplotypes each. 

M x0 y0  C cx y0  cx y1  x1 y1
V y1+15  C dx y1+15  dx y0+15  x0 y0+15  Z

Two properties fall out of that, and they are what the whole renderer rests on.

The vertical profile is smoothstep

Look at the control points of the top curve: (cx, y0) and (cx, y1). Both y-values are just copies of the endpoints. Expand the cubic in y and the control points cancel: 

y(t) = y0(1-t)³ + 3y0(1-t)²t + 3y1(1-t)t² + y1t³
     = y0 + (y1 - y0)·(3t² - 2t³)

3t² − 2t³ is exactly what smoothstep computes. So the vertical motion of a band is one built-in call. There is no bezier evaluation in y at all.

The horizontal sweep never doubles back

The shared control x always lands between 30% and 70% of the way across the band's span — measured, never outside it. That makes x'(t) strictly positive, so x(t) is monotone and each edge is a genuine function of x. That is what makes per-pixel evaluation possible: a pixel's x maps to exactly one point on the curve.

And band thickness is 15 units for every band in the map. The 4,603 <rect> elements are the same primitive with y0 == y1.

Why the pieces are a gift, not a nuisance

The uniformity and the fragmentation are the same fact. A haplotype's real path swoops, flattens, swoops again — an arbitrary curve that would need a genuine tessellator. But the server has already cut it at every segment boundary, and each cut leaves a piece that is exactly one smoothstep transition. The hard decomposition was done upstream. That is why there are no arbitrary beziers left to flatten.

Cutting a shape into pieces normally introduces a new problem: where two pieces meet, each contributes partial coverage to the boundary pixel and blends against the background rather than against its neighbour, leaving a visible hairline at every junction. At 28 to 87 pieces per haplotype that would be a great many hairlines.

It doesn't happen, and the reason is in the data. Across 9,883 junctions in the reference document, every single one overlaps its neighbour by exactly 1.0 unit, with identical y on both sides. The generator laps the pieces rather than butting them. And because lapped pieces belong to the same haplotype and carry the same colour, blending one over the other changes nothing. The seam problem was solved before it reached us.

One consequence worth recording: the piece count is irreducible. Merging consecutive collinear pieces to shrink the instance buffer saves nothing — rectangles and curves strictly alternate, so no haplotype ever has two flat pieces in a row at the same height.

03 Three ways to draw it

With the geometry reduced to six numbers, there are three plausible techniques. They differ in where the curve gets evaluated — on the CPU, in the vertex shader, or in the fragment shader — and that choice sets both the cost and the antialiasing quality.

Why not simply A

At fit scale a band is well under a pixel tall — 0.19 CSS px on the largest document — and hundreds of them are stacked. Four-sample MSAA can only report coverage as 0, ¼, ½, ¾ or 1, where analytic coverage returns the exact fraction. That is what SVG's rasteriser does, and it is the reason the SVG looked right.

Measured, 14 August 2026. Both techniques were built and compared live on one frame. The original text here predicted that MSAA would dissolve the banding into noise. It does not dissolve — it discards. Each of four samples is won outright by a single band, so no background survives and the result looks more saturated than analytic coverage; but at most four of the bands covering a pixel can be represented and the rest are lost. At fit on the largest document, 464 strands land on 177 device rows, and a column carries 101 distinct values under MSAA against 119 under analytic coverage. The conclusion stands; the mechanism was wrong.
And the choice barely matters. At 40× zoom, where a band is 7.7 CSS px, the two techniques differ by 6.18% of pixels at a mean channel delta of 2.32 out of 255 — entirely on band edges. The whole question lives below one pixel per band, which is the regime where hundreds of strands share every pixel row and nothing is legible under any technique. Analytic coverage is kept because it preserves about ten times more distinct colour at no cost, not because the difference is visible where anyone works.

04 The geometry: one mesh, replicated

There is exactly one mesh in the scene: a ladder. It carries no positions — only, for each vertex, a parameter t along the band and a side flag saying whether it belongs to the upper or lower edge.

That ladder is instanced once per band, with the six floats attached as per-instance attributes. The vertex shader places each rung where it belongs: 

// per-instance: x0, y0, x1, y1, cxTop, cxBot
// per-vertex:   t, side

float cx = mix(cxTop, cxBot, side);
float x  = cubicX(x0, cx, cx, x1, t);
float y  = mix(y0, y1, smoothstep(0.0, 1.0, t)) + side * 15.0;

// inflate one device pixel so the coverage
// calculation has somewhere to land
y += (side * 2.0 - 1.0) * pixelSize;

Nothing here touches the CPU. Zooming changes the camera and nothing else — no retessellation, no buffer rebuild, no tolerance to pick. A shared 64-rung ladder over 40,442 bands is about 5 million vertices in one draw call, which is unremarkable work for a GPU.

Rung count, measured 14 August 2026. Sixty-four leaves a worst-case chord error of 0.41 px in x and 0.06 px in y at maximum zoom on the widest piece in the largest document. A sweep of 16, 32, 64 and 128 rungs showed flat frame time throughout, so the count is free in this range, and no evidence emerged that 64 is insufficient. The pixel comparison across that sweep was inconclusive rather than favourable: all three counts differed from 128 by the same fraction, which is camera reproducibility across page loads rather than tessellation.

05 The pixels: coverage, not colour

Corrected 14 August 2026. Placing rungs at even x is what forces an inversion of x(t), because the two edges have different control abscissae and so do not share an x at equal t. Placing them at even t removes the question entirely: both edges reach x0 at t=0 and x1 at t=1 whatever their control abscissae, so sampling both at the same parameter inscribes a correct polygon with vertical ends. The built renderer does that, and has no root-finding in its vertex shader at all. A Newton step survives only in the fragment shader, where the coverage calculation genuinely needs the parameter at the fragment's own x.

Each fragment receives an interpolated t from the rungs on either side. That is close but not exact — the interpolation is linear and the curve is not — so one Newton step against the fragment's true x refines it. Then the shader evaluates both edges at that point and asks a simple question: how much of this pixel is inside the band? 

float yTop = mix(y0, y1, smoothstep(0.0, 1.0, tTop));
float yBot = mix(y0, y1, smoothstep(0.0, 1.0, tBot)) + 15.0;

// overlap between the pixel's vertical extent
// and the band, in device pixels
float half     = 0.5 * fwidth(vY);
float coverage = clamp(min(yBot, vY + half) - max(yTop, vY - half), 0.0, 1.0);

A band covering a fifth of the pixel contributes exactly a fifth. That single line is what preserves the map's appearance at fit scale, and it is why this technique was chosen over the simpler one.

06 Appearance is a table, not a stylesheet

Highlighting one haplotype used to mean swapping a CSS rule, which made the browser recompute style for every element in the map — measured at 28 ms, far past a frame. That was the second reason to leave the DOM.

In the new renderer, each instance carries the strand id it already had in the SVG, and a small texture holds one texel of appearance per strand: RGB for its colour, alpha for how much it is dimmed. The fragment shader reads its own row and multiplies: 

vec4 look = texelFetch(uTrackLUT, ivec2(vTrackID, 0), 0);
outColor   = vec4(look.rgb, look.a * coverage);

Hit-testing comes back the same way it left. A second pass renders strand ids as colours into a one-pixel scissored buffer under the cursor; reading that pixel names the strand. About forty lines, given the attribute already exists.

The DOM renderer's failure was that the picture had to exist somewhere as pixels — a composited layer 35,562 units wide became 900 megapixels at device resolution, and 14.4 gigapixels when zoomed. Here the canvas is the size of the window. There is no oversized surface to tile, so that failure is not fixed so much as made impossible.

08 What would break it

The design rests on a measured claim about someone else's output, and it is worth being explicit about which stone to pull.

  • A band that isn't the canonical shape. The parser is not a path parser; it recognises one grammar and extracts six numbers. Anything else is rejected — the whole document, loudly, falling back to the SVG renderer that still ships alongside. Partial rendering is never offered, because a half-drawn map looks like a correct map of different data.
  • Text. There is none in any of the seventeen documents. It is the hardest thing to draw well in WebGL and it would need a genuinely different answer, not an extension of this one.
  • A control point outside the span. It would make the horizontal sweep non-monotone, a pixel's x would map to two points on the curve, and the coverage calculation loses its meaning. Measured at u ∈ [0.30, 0.70] on every document, so there is real margin — but it is margin, not a guarantee.
  • Butted pieces instead of lapped ones. The 1-unit overlap is the generator's choice, not a property of the format. Remove it and every junction becomes a seam. Worth asserting at parse time, since it would be a subtle, uniform degradation rather than an obvious break.
  • A thickness other than 15. Harmless — it becomes a seventh float. Noted only because so much else follows from the constants.

The first of those is the real exposure. Displaying someone's picture is robust to them changing it; interpreting their geometry is not. That trade is the price of the renderer, and it is paid deliberately: the picture, drawn the old way, no longer appears on screen.

Why not just fix the SVG? Two failures, three days apart, from the same cause: the browser recomputing style for ten thousand elements on hover, and failing to rasterise a layer far past the GPU's maximum texture size. Both are the data defeating a different stage of the same pipeline.

They become geometry, picked with a raycaster; an SVG layer transformed in lockstep with the camera is exactly the coupling this renderer exists to escape.
